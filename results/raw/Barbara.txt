Introduction
• importance of spatial synchrony of populations:
Synchrony of populations abundances is an important phenomenon: synchronous fluctuations
across vast spatial areas have been observed and the underlying causes are not yet fully understood.
These large scale synchrony are important since they increase the risk of extinction when the
abundance of the populations are low. Suppresses the ”rescue effect”.
Two mechanisms have been hypothesized as the potential cause of synchrony: The Moran effect which describes the entrainment by external conditions such as weather and climate, and
density dependent direct interaction between the populations, through dispersal of individuals.
• The influence of dispersal on spatial synchrony has been previously studied:
Populations can be synchronized by even a very small amount of dispersal.
Rate of synchronization needs to be relatively fast which requires population to be either strongly
coupled or weakly coupled populations following relaxation-like oscillations [2].
• Synchronization has also been studied in the case of delayed dispersal: time delay includes the
spatial component of the movement between patches.
Travel-time was shown to have a strong impact on whether populations were able to synchronize
or not. [7].
• However, discrete delay approach assumes that all individuals disperse with a fixed travel-time,
which is not very realistic in ecology where variation between individuals is essential.
• The importance of heterogeneity in dispersal has also been an important research subject. reviewed by Colin Hawkes [3]
Dispersal distances are found to have leptokurtic distributions and these distributions are thought
to be caused by intra-specific variation.
More realistic inclusion of dispersal in metapopulation models has a huge influence on the dynamics of populations [4].
• We are interested in how individual variation in inter-patch movement might influence population synchrony.
• Following Wall et al, we include movement behavior in a two patch Rozensweig-MacArthur model
as a time delay but replacing the discrete delay by a distributed delay.

Analysis of Synchronization states in the ODE model
Using the linear chain technique allowed us to convert the model with delay differential equations
into a model with ordinary differential equations. We use the continuation software Matcont [1] to
find the periodic orbits for specific p and τ values.
The stability of a periodic orbit is determined by the associated Floquet multipliers mi , i = 1, ..., (4+
2p) computed by the software. On a periodic orbit, there is always a multiplier m1 =1. The orbit is
stable if |mi | < 1 for i = 2, ..., (4 + 2p) and unstable otherwise. Fixing p we can then vary τ to find the
bifurcation points where periodic orbits gain or lose stability.
We are interested in the α−µ−ε parameter region where the dynamics of the Rosenzweig-MacArthur
model oscillate on a stable limit cycle. This happens when α < 1 and µ < 1−α
1+α [2]. Moreover, we are interested in the region where these oscillations are relaxation-like, since this leads to faster convergence
to synchrony. Here we focus on the parameter set α = 0.35, µ = 0.3 and ε = 0.1.

The system can exhibit three different phase-locking regimes for our parameter values, each corresponding to a different periodic orbit. We refer to the system as exhibiting In-Phase, Anti-Phase
and Out-of-Phase synchrony when the solutions oscillate on periodic orbits with a phase difference

of φ = 0, φ = π and 0 < φ < π respectively. The shape of the stable periodic orbit in the h1 − h2 plane
determines the phase-locked regime of the solutions (Fig. 2): A straight line (yellow orbit) means that
h1 and h2 reach their maximum and minimum at the same time, i.e. that the oscillations are In-Phase
synchronous. The blue orbit is shaped such that when h1 attains its maximum, h2 is at its minimum
and vice-versa, which corresponds to Anti-Phase behavior. The third orbit (black) can take different
shapes each corresponding to a specific phase difference between 0 and π.
For our bifurcation analysis, we choose d = 0.001, which corresponds to a weak coupling strength
[7].
In a single-patch system with these parameters, the populations oscillate with period T = 7.34 [2].
Since we assume no mortality during movement which means that travel-time is limited by individual
lifetime, we decide to focus on varying the mean travel-time τ from 0 to T .

3

Results

3.1

Phase Locked States

The mean delay and the variance strongly affects the number and stability of periodic orbits in the system. We find two periodic orbits present throughout the entire range of τ corresponding to Anti-Phase
and In-Phase behavior (Fig. 3. As we vary τ from 0 to 7.34, these periodic orbits change in stability and
an additional Out-of-Phase orbit is formed at Branch Points (red stars) and Fold bifurcation points
(red dots).
For each fixed p value, the variance changes quadratically with the mean τ (σ 2 = τ 2 /p). Therefore,
for the full range of τ we obtain different ranges of variance for each p. We notice that the system’s
behavior is similar for small τ values, with three bifurcation points common to all diagrams. However,
for smaller variance σ 2 (i.e. higher p values), the system presents additional branch points at higher
τ , at which the anti-phase orbit loses stability and the in-phase orbit eventually stabilizes again. This
behavior is similar to what happens in the case of a discrete time delay [7], although the transition
between phase-locking states appears to be smoother as there is no hysteresis or bi-stability.

We observe that for small mean delay τ , the stable phase-locked states are consistent with previous
studies on synchronization by discrete time delay dispersal [7]. The range of delay for which the system can be synchronized by weak dispersal is not affected by small individual variation: The in-phase
synchrony orbit loses stability for τ = 0.16 ± 0.02 from which point only Out-of-Phase dynamics are
possible until τ = 0.42 ± 0.01 where the anti-phase orbit stabilizes.
However, for higher τ values, we find that the variance has a strong influence on the stability of
the phase-locked states. For high enough variation (σ 2 > 10), the system completely loses the ability to synchronize. Additionally, we notice that as the variance increases, the range of τ for which
the in-phase periodic orbit is stable decreases. Overall, the region of the τ − σ 2 where the population abundances oscillate in phase appears to be small in comparison to the other synchronization
regimes.

3.2

Convergence to Synchrony

Computing the phase difference from simulations of the ordinary differential equations model 10, we
can extract the rate of convergence to synchrony in the region where the solutions converge exponentially to the stable phase-locked orbit.
Previous studies have shown that the rate of convergence to In-Phase synchrony is smaller than
the rate of convergence to Anti-Phase synchrony [2] and that a time delay significantly affects the rate
of convergence [7]. We are interested in how adding heterogeneity to the system compares to these
results.
7

Figure 5: Rate of Convergence to stable periodic orbit as a function of the variance for different mean
delays τ . Values of τ for which anti-phase is stable (a), bifurcations are present (b) and in-phase is
stable (c).
We find that for values of τ and σ 2 far enough from any bifurcation points, the rate of convergence
decreases with increase in variance (Fig. 5 a and b). Moreover, it appears that the rate of convergence
to In-phase is overall smaller than the rate of convergence to Anti-Phase.
For high enough variance, rate of convergence to In-Phase synchrony is too small to be significant,
while this is not the case for convergence to anti-phase.
However, there are some limiting cases (see Figure 5b): because the rate of convergence is zero at
any bifurcation points and that bifurcation points occur at different values of τ , the relationship between variance and rate of convergence is different when the mean delay is close to the bifurcation
points. This makes comparison between small and large delays more complicated since the bifurcation points make the relationship between variance and rate of convergence non-monotonic.

Discussion
• Introduce a mathematical technique that allows for a more convenient way to study distributeddelays. Distributed delays are useful to ecology since variation is an essential feature of populations.
Gamma distribution is suitable to model travel-time.
• Heterogeneity in the travel time leads to population persistence:
High variance prevents synchronous behavior for small enough travel time and therefore reduces risk of extinction.
High variance also decreases the rate of convergence to synchrony, meaning that for significant
variation, dispersal is not likely to synchronize the patches.
• Distributed time delay and patch dynamics:
Allow to bring together important concepts in ecology: metapopulation ecology and patch dynamics, importance of landscape matrix and importance of heterogeneity in dispersal movement.
